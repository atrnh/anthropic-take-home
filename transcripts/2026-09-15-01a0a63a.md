## ChatGPT

For this kind of site, I would start with **a map of user tasks and product differences, then test competing navigation structures**. Similar feature names alone don’t tell you whether the documentation should live together.

The crucial distinction is between **audience overlap and task overlap**. Someone might use two products for unrelated work. Conversely, people with different job titles might need exactly the same instructions.

Below are research-backed methods and architectural patterns, with my recommendations for applying them to your situation.

## 1. Map what is actually shared

### Exercise: build a task × product matrix

Start with a representative content inventory. NN/g distinguishes an inventory, which records what content exists, from an audit, which evaluates its quality and usefulness. For your site, extend the inventory to record where product behavior diverges. [Content inventory and auditing](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/content-audits/)

Use user tasks as rows and products as columns. Here is a hypothetical example:

| User task | Product A | Product B | Product C | Documentation implication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Understand access permissions | Same model | Same model | Different model | Shared explanation for A/B; separate explanation for C |
| Invite a collaborator | Email invitation | Email invitation | Managed through an identity provider | Similar goal, different procedures |
| Export a report | CSV or PDF | CSV only | Unsupported | Explicit availability and format differences |
| Connect A to B | Starts in A | Finishes in B | Not applicable | Guide spanning both products |

For each row, record differences in:

- Meaning and terminology.
- Prerequisites, permissions, and assumed knowledge.
- Steps and interface.
- Results, limits, and availability.

**The output is a decision about content boundaries.** A shared concept might deserve one explanation even when each product needs its own procedure. A shared feature name might conceal entirely different concepts.

My suggested rule: combine content when readers can use it without repeatedly interpreting exceptions. Split it when readers must keep translating the page into their own product’s behavior.

## 2. Find out whether people think in products, tasks, or workflows

### Exercise: interview around recent documentation needs

Ask participants to reconstruct a real occasion when they needed help:

- What were they trying to accomplish?
- What did they already know about the product or feature?
- What words did they search for?
- Did the task involve another product?
- How did they decide that the answer applied to their situation?

Recruit across three kinds of usage:

1. People who use only one product.
2. People who use several products independently.
3. People whose work crosses product boundaries.

Include differences in experience and responsibilities where they affect the work. Don’t assume a job title predicts documentation needs.

Then prioritize the tasks that recur. Gerry McGovern’s **Top Tasks** method provides a more formal approach to identifying what matters most to users and measuring whether they can complete those tasks. [Top Tasks methodology](https://gerrymcgovern.com/books/top-tasks-a-how-to-guide/read-the-first-chapter/)

For your situation, I would examine priorities within each usage group before combining them. Otherwise, a large product’s audience can obscure an essential task for a smaller product.

### Exercise: map one workflow across products

Choose a task involving multiple products and map:

> Trigger → first action → product transition → next action → successful outcome

At each transition, note what users must carry forward: credentials, an identifier, configuration, vocabulary, or an understanding of what just happened.

This is a useful way to discover documentation that belongs to the **workflow itself**. Neither product’s standalone documentation may provide a complete answer.

## 3. Use card sorting to discover groupings

In an open card sort, participants group representative content items and name the groups. It helps reveal their expectations about what belongs together. NN/g recommends roughly 30–50 cards, piloting the labels, and asking about difficult or ambiguous placements. [Card-sorting guidance](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/card-sorting-definition/)

For your site:

- Use concrete topics or tasks, such as “Invite someone to a workspace.”
- Include shared, product-specific, and cross-product material.
- Let people set aside unfamiliar items.
- Ask which items could belong in more than one group.
- Analyze patterns by product usage before looking at the aggregate.

Be careful with product names on cards. Repeating “Product A” across many labels can encourage people to group by that wording. NN/g explicitly cautions against repeated words that bias grouping. Retain product context when necessary to understand an item, but don’t build your preferred hierarchy into every label.

**The output should be candidate groupings and vocabulary.** Card sorting does not automatically produce a usable sitemap.

## 4. Compare these architectural patterns

These are design hypotheses to test against your task and audience research.

| Pattern | Most promising when | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Product first**: product → task/topic | People arrive knowing their product, and procedures differ substantially | Shared concepts and workflows become difficult to discover across product sections |
| **Task or capability first**: task/topic → product | Users recognize a common goal more readily than product boundaries | Readers may reach instructions for the wrong product |
| **Shared topic with a product selector** | Products have a close correspondence between topics, with bounded differences | Hidden selection state or missing variants can cause confusion |
| **Product sections plus cross-product guides** | Single-product work and integrated workflows are both important | Shared guides can become an unstructured miscellaneous section |
| **Audience landing pages** linking into the same content | Particular groups need distinct starting points or curated learning paths | People who fit several groups may not know where to begin |

### Be cautious about audience-first navigation

NN/g’s research identifies recurring problems when people must choose an audience category: they may belong to several groups, misunderstand the labels, or worry that useful information exists elsewhere. It recommends task/topic organization and using audience sections where the content is sufficiently distinct. [Audience-based navigation](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/audience-based-navigation/)

For overlapping product audiences, I would usually test **optional audience landing pages** before making audience selection the gateway to all documentation.

### A relevant example: GitHub Docs

GitHub lets readers select documentation for their plan or product context. Its own guidance explicitly recognizes that one person may use GitHub.com and an Enterprise Server instance, and need different versions of the same article at different times. That closely matches your overlapping-user scenario. [About versions of GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/get-started/using-github-docs/about-versions-of-github-docs)

The useful principle is that **context belongs to the current task**. A person’s identity does not necessarily determine which documentation they need today.

## 5. Test navigation separately from page comprehension

### Exercise: tree-test two or three candidate structures

A tree test presents the hierarchy as text and asks participants where they would go to complete a task. It evaluates labels and placement without visual design. NN/g recommends using card sorting to generate possibilities and tree testing to evaluate them. [Tree-testing guidance](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tree-testing/)

Test scenarios such as:

- “You use Product B and need to give a colleague access.”
- “You know how exports work in Product A. Find out whether Product B supports the same format.”
- “You need data created in A to become available in B.”
- “Find out whether this feature is available in your product.”

Record success, directness, backtracking, and wrong-product destinations. Break results down by usage group. An overall success rate can hide a structure that works well for one group and poorly for another.

Avoid naming the expected navigation label in the task prompt. Pilot the tasks before a larger study.

### Exercise: test the same feature in three page formats

A successful tree test doesn’t establish that someone can interpret the page correctly. Choose a feature with meaningful differences and prototype:

1. One shared page with labeled product sections.
2. One topic with a product selector.
3. Separate product pages with links to the corresponding topic elsewhere.

Ask participants to find the answer **and explain which product, prerequisites, and limitations it applies to**.

For people using multiple products, add a comparison task. A selector may work well for following instructions while making side-by-side comparison cumbersome.

Also test arrival directly from a search result. My recommendation is that readers should be able to identify the page’s product context without having visited the homepage or made an earlier selection.

## 6. Separate the navigation model from the content model

A topic can have several useful routes to it without requiring several independently maintained copies.

For example, one explanation of a genuinely shared permission model could be linked from:

- Product A’s access documentation.
- Product B’s access documentation.
- An administration overview.

The source may also reuse common material while presenting complete product-specific pages. **Content reuse is an authoring decision; navigation is a reader decision.** Treating them separately gives you more options.

Diátaxis adds another useful dimension: tutorials, how-to guides, reference, and explanation serve different needs. [Diátaxis](https://diataxis.fr/)

I would use those distinctions to evaluate content within your product/topic structure. For instance:

- A shared explanation describes the permission model.
- Separate how-to guides cover each product’s configuration.
- A comparison reference lists supported roles.
- A cross-product tutorial teaches an integrated workflow.

That is an application of Diátaxis to your scenario, rather than a claim that its four categories must become your site’s top-level navigation.

## Where I would start

For an initial investigation, I would use this sequence:

1. **Choose a representative slice** containing a shared concept, a divergent procedure, a product-exclusive feature, and a cross-product workflow.
2. **Build the task × product matrix** and gather real user scenarios.
3. **Run an open card sort** if the expected groupings and labels are still uncertain.
4. **Sketch competing trees**, especially product-first and product sections with cross-product guides.
5. **Tree-test the routes**, then test whether readers understand applicability on the destination pages.

My starting hypothesis would be **product sections with consistent task labels, shared explanations where the concepts truly match, and explicit guides for work spanning products**. I would move toward a capability-first structure if research showed that users consistently approach the site through shared tasks and can reliably identify the product-specific answer.

The deciding evidence is whether people can find the right answer, recognize that it applies to them, and move between products when their work requires it.

For shared content, you could establish “Applies to web and desktop” in the page template or section introduction. Avoid repeating it throughout every procedure.

## One distinction to check

Make sure “local files” describes a meaningful difference to users. If the web app supports uploading a file, distinguish that from desktop access to a folder or ongoing access to local files. Likewise, explain what “run local commands” enables in task terms, such as running a project’s tests, alongside whatever permission and execution behavior actually exists.

That may create separate documentation for those workflows, but it doesn’t require splitting the whole product’s documentation.

My test would be simple: **can a web user follow a shared page without encountering unavailable steps, and can a desktop user find the local capabilities without hunting?** If both hold, the merged structure is doing its job.
