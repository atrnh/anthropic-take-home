# Theory: functional quality vs deep quality

Paraphrased from Daniele Procida, "Towards a theory of quality in documentation", Diátaxis
(https://diataxis.fr/quality/). Read that page for the original argument.

## Why distinguish them

We rarely define "quality". Still, people can point to excellent documentation and tend to
agree when they see lapses. That shared intuition is worth sharpening. Splitting quality into
two kinds makes it something you can discuss and act on.

## Functional quality

Accuracy, completeness, consistency, usefulness, precision, and similar standards. Missing any
one of them means a key function is failing.

- The dimensions are **independent**. Writing can be accurate but incomplete. It can be
  complete but inaccurate and inconsistent. It can be all of these and still useless.
- They are **objective** and sometimes literally countable (completeness).
- Meeting them takes discipline, attention to detail, and technical skill. Any failure is
  **obvious to the reader**.

## Deep quality

Feeling good to use, having flow, fitting human needs, being beautiful, anticipating the user.

- You **can't check or measure** these, but you can clearly identify them. Most people
  recognise them when they meet them.
- Writing can pass every functional test and still be awkward and unpleasant. Accurate,
  consistent, even useful material can lack deep quality.

I've finished the review. The memo's claims mostly hold up against the corpus. What holds it back is structure: the same ideas come back in three or four places, and the second half drops from a decision memo into implementation detail.

## Verdict
It's mostly accurate: most claims I checked are right, with two overstatements and one self-contradiction. The first half has real flow. The biggest problem is that the memo doesn't follow its own "explain each concept once" rule. Diátaxis, context scoping and old-link handling each come up several times, and their priority order conflicts with the supporting draft.

## Reader and need
**The reader** is a hiring panel or docs lead who reads this first ([README.md](README.md) says "Start here"). They're evaluating, not in a hurry, and they're skeptical. Their question is: *is this diagnosis right, and does this person have good judgement about what matters?* Behind them is the imagined stakeholder, whose question is *should we approve this restructure?*

**The mode** is a decision memo, which is explanation plus a plan. The first half serves that reader. From "What to merge" on, it slides into an implementation spec for engineers: anchors, `llms.txt`, event names. The modes are mixed.

## Functional quality

**Accuracy**
- **Line 44, overstated.** "in Government, an installed plugin doesn't connect its bundled tools." The source ([government/desktop/plugins.md:40](corpus/government/desktop/plugins.md:40)) is narrower: connectors declared by a plugin *you add yourself* aren't added, and local MCP servers never run. Admin-provided connectors still work. **Fix:** "a plugin you upload yourself doesn't add its connectors."
- **Lines 62–65, partly unfair.** "Skills have the same gap" suggests the two pages contradict each other. They don't: the Government page reconciles them explicitly at [skills.md:39](corpus/government/desktop/skills.md:39). ## Deep quality
**The reading experience.** The opening lands. It makes a crisp claim, backs it with a concrete observation, and states the move. Sections 2–5 keep the rhythm: principle, then a linked example. Then "Principles behind the proposal" restates those five improvements in new words and adds rules that are really implementation detail, like shared-link precedence and "How to check" placement. The reader gets déjà vu and starts to wonder whether they missed a distinction. The nav tree, the most concrete proof of improvement #1, arrives a full screen after the section it proves. "What to merge" opens with redirect mechanics before it says what's being merged. The same anchor and redirect caveat appears at lines 163, 172, 179–181, 192 and 209.

**Root causes**
1. **The README is organised around the document set, not the reader's decision path.** It condenses each supporting draft in turn (audit, IA, ledger, plugin example), so every section re-introduces the shared ideas in its own voice. The memo recommends one home per concept and doesn't practise it. A sharp reviewer will notice.
2. **It mixes altitudes.** Strategic judgement and implementation spec are interleaved, so the reader can't tell which details they're meant to evaluate.

**Specific breaks and fixes**
- **"Principles behind the proposal," lines 80–108.** Cut this section. Fold its two new ideas, keeping context choices distinct and not claiming more than the evidence supports, into improvement #4 and a one-line evidence note at the top. "Don't claim more than the evidence supports" is one of your best lines, and right now it's buried.
- **"Proposed navigation."** Move the tree directly under improvement #1 as its evidence. Then the memo reads as *problem → shape of the fix → how to get there → how to know*.
- **"What to merge," lines 161–181.** Lead with the table, preceded by one sentence on the principle ("no reader loses their destination"). Put every routing caveat in a single sentence after it.
- **The opening.** Add a three-line summary before the first heading covering the thesis, the scope (54 pages, snapshot date) and the top recommendation. It answers the reviewer's first question before they ask it.

## What already works
- **Imperative headings that are themselves the recommendations.** A skimmer gets the whole argument from the outline.
- **Every claim paired with a linked corpus example.** The GitHub files vs. GitHub tools contrast is especially clean.
- **"Sharing a product name doesn't make two tasks the same"** and **"installed" vs. "ready to use"** are memorable reframings that keep working as design rules.
- **"A missing procedure doesn't mean it's unsupported"** shows epistemic care that a reviewer will trust.

- **"A page view doesn't tell us the task is done."** The measurement section measures outcomes, not traffic.
- **The supporting-drafts list** gives each link a one-line job.
