1	# Theory: functional quality vs deep quality
2
3	Paraphrased from Daniele Procida, "Towards a theory of quality in documentation", Diátaxis
4	(https://diataxis.fr/quality/). Read that page for the original argument.
5
6	## Why distinguish them
7
8	We rarely define "quality". Still, people can point to excellent documentation and tend to
9	agree when they see lapses. That shared intuition is worth sharpening. Splitting quality into
10	two kinds makes it something you can discuss and act on.
11
12	## Functional quality
13
14	Accuracy, completeness, consistency, usefulness, precision, and similar standards. Missing any
15	one of them means a key function is failing.
16
17	- The dimensions are **independent**. Writing can be accurate but incomplete. It can be
18	  complete but inaccurate and inconsistent. It can be all of these and still useless.
19	- They are **objective** and sometimes literally countable (completeness).
20	- Meeting them takes discipline, attention to detail, and technical skill. Any failure is
21	  **obvious to the reader**.
22
23	## Deep quality
24
25	Feeling good to use, having flow, fitting human needs, being beautiful, anticipating the user.
26
27	- You **can't check or measure** these, but you can clearly identify them. Most people
28	  recognise them when they meet them.
29	- Writing can pass every functional test and still be awkward and unpleasant. Accurate,
30	  consistent, even useful material can lack deep quality.

## What the paragraph says now

> This is usually how I win over teams that ignore me. It usually comes down to a lack or loss of trust that needs to be won over again. They need to know that engagement is worth the time and effort.

It makes three moves: teams ignore you, the cause is trust, and reliability wins it back. Each one leaves a gap.

## Gap 1: it assumes the cause

Trust is one reason a team ignores a standard, but not the only one. The causes need different responses, and some of them mean the team is right:

| Cause | What it looks like | What you'd do |
|---|---|---|
| **Noisy check** | Flags get dismissed or ignored | Fix the tool; the team is giving you accurate feedback |
| **Unclear owner** | Nobody responds because nobody thinks it's theirs | Find the owner first; there's nobody to persuade yet |
| **Deadline pressure** | "Not now" | Time it better, and make the smallest ask that still helps readers |
| **Legitimate exception** | The standard doesn't fit their content | Agree a scoped exception, or change the standard |
| **Lost trust** | Past promises from docs weren't kept | Reliability and follow-through (what you have now) |

Naming these shows the panel that you'd diagnose before you persuade. That's the same move as "get to know people, not to persuade", so the page stays consistent with itself. It also heads off a reviewer's worry that you'd treat valid objections as attitude problems.

## Gap 2: "win back trust" has no concrete moves

"Engagement is worth the time and effort" is the goal, but the reader never sees the moves. Some concrete ones:
- Fix what they complained about, then tell them you did. Closing that loop matters more than the fix itself.
- Do the first update with them: pair on one page instead of sending them the template.
- Shrink the ask: one page, not a migration.
- Go where they already work (their channel, their PR review) instead of asking them to come to you.

One real anecdote from your curriculum or build-system work would do more here than any list. Something like "a team that had stopped answering my messages started engaging after I…". The current line "This is usually how I win over teams" makes that claim without the evidence.

## Gap 3: no plan for when it fails

This is the part evaluators will probe. If trust-building doesn't work and readers are still being hurt (duplicated, conflicting, or stale instructions in plugin docs), what then? Your previous version had a good answer:

> If a reader-facing problem persists, document its impact and ask the responsible product owner to agree on a fix and a date. Escalate the unresolved reader impact when necessary, rather than treating compliance itself as the goal.

That last clause is the strongest idea in either version. You escalate the harm to readers, not the team's non-compliance. It keeps you credible with teams because you're not the style police, and it gives leadership something they care about.

## Two smaller points
- **Ignoring the check isn't the same as ignoring the standard.** The check is advisory by design, so a team dismissing its flags is data about the tool, not defiance. Saying so shows you understand your own system.
- **Not every holdout needs winning.** Triage by reader impact: a low-traffic page that doesn't follow the template may not be worth the relationship cost.
