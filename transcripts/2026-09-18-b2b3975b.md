Paraphrased from Daniele Procida, "Towards a theory of quality in documentation", Diátaxis

## Why distinguish them

We rarely define "quality". Still, people can point to excellent documentation and tend to
agree when they see lapses. That shared intuition is worth sharpening. Splitting quality into
two kinds makes it something you can discuss and act on.

## Functional quality

Accuracy, completeness, consistency, usefulness, precision, and similar standards. Missing any
one of them means a key function is failing.

- The dimensions are **independent**. Writing can be accurate but incomplete. It can be
  complete but inaccurate and inconsistent. It can be all of these and still useless.
- They are **objective** and sometimes literally countable (completeness).
- Meeting them takes discipline, attention to detail, and technical skill. Any failure is
  **obvious to the reader**.

## Deep quality

Feeling good to use, having flow, fitting human needs, being beautiful, anticipating the user.

- You **can't check or measure** these, but you can clearly identify them. Most people
  recognise them when they meet them.
- Writing can pass every functional test and still be awkward and unpleasant. Accurate,
  consistent, even useful material can lack deep quality.

## Verdict

The README is accurate and very candid, but it doesn't read as a story yet. It's a product README with a research log attached to the end. The retrieval half of the history is scattered in pieces, told backwards, or left out, and the policy conclusions appear before the experiments that produced them. The biggest problem is order. The reader gets the conclusions (policy, results table, "advice stays optional") before the events that earned them, and then gets them again in the history.

## Reader and need

The main reader is a hiring reviewer assessing Part 3. They're curious and critical, and they're reading for judgment and process more than to run the tool (`usage.md` covers running it). What they need is an **explanation**: what I tried, what each step taught me, and why the tool ended up this way. A second reader is the practitioner who wants to know what the tool does and whether to trust it.

The document mixes these modes. Lines 1–141 are README/reference material for the practitioner. Lines 143–289 are the explanation for the evaluator. The two halves repeat each other.

## Functional quality

**Accuracy**
- Line 193: "missed five expected consolidations… Those misses included the plugin definitions, sales example, skill definition, and slug explanation." A reader counts four. `judge-v2/README.md:22` says "*both* plugin definitions". Write "both Government plugin definitions" so the count adds up.
- Everything else I checked matches the pilot artifacts: v1 14/16 (5 TP + 9 TN), v3 5/6 with ten TN, v4 2/3/1 with Drive treated as negative, v5 3/6 and 4/6, the coverage table, and the 18/3/15 baseline.

**Completeness**
- **The judge model is never named.** `judge-v5/README.md` records GPT-5.6-Terra at high effort. Given who's reading, leaving it out is conspicuous. State it and say why you chose it, or why the model doesn't matter.

The switch to TF-IDF, the core design choice, gets one definitional sentence (line 27) and no reason.

That fact is the hinge of the v4 plot twist, where the judge's "false positives" turned out to be label errors. Introduce it when you introduce the evaluation set.

## Deep quality

Reading as the reviewer: the first 40 lines are clear. Then line 42 presents a consolidation policy as settled fact, and at line 56 the reader first hears that "the human review established" it, with no way to know that experiments produced it. The table at line 76 gives v5 scores and fresh-case retrieval before the reader knows v5, fresh cases, or MCPB exist, so they have to take it on trust. Lines 130–134 bring up the lexical baseline almost as an aside. The actual narrative starts only at line 143, where the reader realizes they've already been told the ending three times. The retrieval story then arrives at line 272 as a coda, even though it happened first.

**Root cause 1: the order follows the current artifact, not the order in which the knowledge was gained.** Every conclusion shows up before its evidence, and the evidence repeats it. That's why the text feels long and redundant. The same claims recur:
- "Advice is optional and can't filter the queue" appears three times (lines 37–40, 96–98, 279–282).
- "Small, tuned sets, not general accuracy" appears four times (86–91, 94–96, 102, 284–289).
- The maintenance and threshold text duplicates `pilot/README.md` almost word for word.

**Root cause 2: only half the project is told as a story.** The judge gets five careful subsections. Retrieval, which went through three stages (lexical → TF-IDF → expanded extraction → checklists + top-20), gets fragments. The real arc is two problems that the first failure split apart. The lexical checker failed at *finding* candidates and at *judging* them. You then worked on those two problems separately, and they came back together in the final design: broad retrieval plus a human decision. That shape is the story, and it's currently invisible.
